The science

Reading the optical
fingerprint of tissue

A spot of fluorescence can look bright or faint for several reasons. Its spectrum carries information about the molecules that emitted the light and the tissue it traveled through.

A spectrum at each pixel

A color camera records only red, green, and blue. However, a hyperspectral camera measures light across many wavelengths at each pixel. That pattern is the optical fingerprint of the tissue at that point in the surgical field. In fluorescence-guided glioma surgery, one signal of interest comes from protoporphyrin IX (PpIX), which emits red light when excited by blue light.

The challenge in surgery is capturing enough light to measure faint fluorescence while preserving detail and keeping pace with a moving field. OncoLight captures the spectrum at every pixel in a single exposure for high-resolution imaging in real time. We’re developing the optics and software together to preserve both the detail of the surgical view and the spectral differences that distinguish one signal from another.

Our surgeons and engineers design these molecular imaging tools together. What surgeons need to see during an operation guides how we build the technology and fit it into the surgical workflow.

Finding a molecule’s contribution

The red light measured at a single pixel can include both PpIX fluorescence and the tissue’s autofluorescence. Each has a characteristic emission pattern. Our software compares the measured spectrum with those patterns to estimate how much each source contributes. Repeating that calculation across the image produces a map of PpIX fluorescence.

A weak signal can have more than one explanation. There may be less PpIX, or blood may absorb some of the light before it reaches the camera. Tissue scattering also changes the paths light takes. By measuring reflected light alongside fluorescence, we can account for these effects and better estimate how much of the signal comes from PpIX itself. That distinction matters at a glioma margin, where the visible glow can be faint.

Hyperspectral imaging in neurosurgery

Our founding team reviewed the cameras and computational methods used to measure reflected light and fluorescence in neurosurgery. The review explains how camera design affects imaging speed, the detail visible across the field, and the wavelengths a system can distinguish.

  • Review of hyperspectral systems and methodsKotwal et al. · Journal of Biomedical Optics · 2025

Measuring fluorescence with a probe

A fiber-optic probe measures the spectrum at the point where it touches tissue. An early study used that spectrum to estimate protoporphyrin IX (PpIX) concentration during brain tumor surgery. A follow-up study in low-grade glioma compared the probe measurement with the fluorescence visible to the surgeon.

  • Quantitative PpIX measurements in brain tumor surgeryValdés et al. · Journal of Neurosurgery · 2011
  • Probe spectroscopy in low-grade gliomaValdés et al. · Journal of Neurosurgery · 2015

Combining fluorescence and reflectance

The probe also measures light reflected from the same tissue. Researchers used the two signals together to estimate PpIX concentration, hemoglobin oxygenation, and light scattering. More recently, the team compared paired spectra with histology to study how these signals vary across glioma tissue.

  • Measuring glioma biomarkers with a combined probeValdés et al. · Journal of Biomedical Optics · 2011
  • Paired spectra and histology in glioma surgeryChiwo et al. · Neuro-Oncology · 2026

Mapping fluorescence across the surgical field

Wide-field imaging extends the measurement from one point to an entire image. The researchers estimated fluorophore concentration at each pixel and used reflected light to correct for absorption and scattering in tissue models.

  • Quantitative fluorescence imaging at each pixelValdés et al. · Scientific Reports · 2012
  • Wide-field quantitative fluorescence imaging during neurosurgeryValdés et al. · Optics Letters · 2013
